Topic: Web-based Customer Experience Management Application for Energy Utility

Participants:
	•	Rajiv Mehta (RM) – IT Manager, Energy Utility Company
	•	Anita Sharma (AS) – Sales Representative, IT Consulting Firm

AS:
Good morning, Rajiv. Thanks for taking the time today. Hope you're doing well.

RM:
Good morning, Anita. Doing well, thanks. Hope the same with you.

AS:
All good here as well. Before we start, just to align—this call is mainly a discovery session. We'd like to understand your current challenges, future vision, and priorities so that we can come back with a meaningful solution rather than something generic.

RM:
That sounds perfect. We want to make sure we're solving the right problems.

AS:
Exactly. I'll ask a lot of questions, some business-focused and some technical. Please feel free to interrupt or add context anytime.

RM:
Sure, go ahead.

AS:
To begin with, could you give me a brief overview of your organization and your role?

RM:
Sure. We're a regional electricity distribution utility. We serve residential consumers, commercial establishments, and a few small industrial units.
I head IT applications, which includes customer-facing systems, billing integrations, and internal operational tools.

AS:
Got it. And what are the key challenges that pushed you to think about a new customer experience platform?

RM:
Customer expectations have changed a lot. People expect digital-first services now.
Our problem is that customer interactions are scattered—new connections are handled one way, complaints another way, outages mostly through call centers. There's no single system that gives a unified view of the customer.

AS:
So both customers and internal teams feel the pain?

RM:
Exactly. Customers feel we're slow and opaque. Internally, teams struggle because data is spread across systems and even spreadsheets.

AS:
So today, customers interact through multiple channels—call center, emails, walk-in offices?

RM:
Yes. And each channel operates almost independently. A customer might log a complaint via email, then call the helpline, and the agent has no visibility of the email interaction.

AS:
That leads to repeated explanations and frustration.

RM:
Absolutely. And it reflects badly on us, even if the actual resolution is happening behind the scenes.

AS:
Understood. So the goal is one centralized, web-based platform for all customer interactions.

RM:
Yes. A single source of truth for customer engagement.

AS:
When you think about "customer experience management," what does an ideal future state look like?

RM:
From a customer's perspective, they should be able to do everything online—apply for a new connection, check application status, pay fees, raise complaints, track outages, and receive updates without calling us.

AS:
And internally?

RM:
Internally, we want visibility. For any customer, we should see their entire journey—applications, complaints, outages faced, payments, communication history.
This helps us improve service and also respond better when a customer escalates.

AS:
So transparency, speed, and consistency.

RM:
Exactly.

AS:
Let's deep dive into new connection requests. How does this work today?

RM:
Customers usually fill out a physical form or download a PDF. They submit documents at our office. Payments are done at a counter or via separate portals.
After that, the application moves through multiple departments—engineering, field inspection, approvals—mostly via emails or internal notes.

AS:
That sounds time-consuming and error-prone.

RM:
It is. Customers keep calling to ask, "Where is my application stuck?"

AS:
What would you like to see in the new system?

RM:
A guided digital journey. The system should validate inputs, allow document uploads, calculate fees automatically, and support online payments.
Once submitted, the application should move through predefined stages with ownership clearly defined.

AS:
Would customers be able to track each stage?

RM:
Yes. Even a simple progress tracker—submitted, under verification, inspection scheduled, approved, meter installed—would make a huge difference.

AS:
And SLAs?

RM:
Very important. Residential connections within 7 days, commercial within maybe 15.
If something breaches SLA, supervisors should get alerts automatically.

AS:
Let's talk about incidents and outages.

RM:
This is where we face the most pressure. During outages, our call center gets overwhelmed.

AS:
How do customers currently report outages?

RM:
Mostly via calls. Some emails. Very few digital options.

AS:
What's your expectation from the new platform?

RM:
Customers should be able to report outages via web or mobile.
But more importantly, for large outages, they shouldn't have to report at all—the system should proactively show that an outage is already known.

AS:
That requires integration with grid or SCADA systems.

RM:
Yes. Even if it's phase two, we want the architecture to support it.

AS:
And visibility to customers?

RM:
Status updates—outage identified, crew assigned, estimated restoration time. This alone will reduce calls by 50%.

AS:
Apart from outages, what other service requests are common?

RM:
Billing disputes, incorrect meter readings, meter faults, voltage fluctuation complaints, name changes, load enhancement requests.

AS:
Do these follow different processes internally?

RM:
Yes. Some are finance-driven, some require field visits, some need approvals.
The system should support different workflows without custom coding every time.

AS:
So a configurable workflow engine would be ideal.

RM:
Exactly.

AS:
How do you currently communicate updates to customers?

RM:
Mostly SMS and email, but it's triggered manually or through batch jobs.

AS:
What would an ideal communication model look like?

RM:
Event-driven notifications. If status changes, customer gets notified automatically.
Also, a portal inbox where customers can see all messages in one place.

AS:
Would you also want multilingual support?

RM:
Yes, at least English and the local language.

AS:
Let's talk about internal users.

RM:
We'll have call center agents, field technicians, supervisors, department managers, and system administrators.

AS:
Role-based access is critical then.

RM:
Yes. A technician shouldn't see billing details. Managers should see KPIs, not operational clutter.

AS:
Understood.

AS:
What kind of insights are important for leadership?

RM:
Complaint trends, outage frequency, SLA compliance, customer satisfaction scores, regional performance comparisons.

AS:
Do you want real-time dashboards or periodic reports?

RM:
Both. Real-time dashboards during outages or peak demand, and scheduled reports for reviews.

AS:
Any non-functional expectations we should be aware of?

RM:
Scalability is critical. During major outages, traffic can spike 10x.
Availability also—downtime during a crisis is unacceptable.

AS:
And security?

RM:
Strong role-based access, audit logs, encryption, and compliance with data protection regulations.

AS:
Which existing systems should this platform integrate with?

RM:
Billing, payment gateways, GIS, outage management, possibly CRM.

AS:
Are these modern systems?

RM:
A mix. Some expose APIs, some are legacy. We expect phased integration.

AS:
Any preference for deployment?

RM:
Cloud-first, but compliant and secure. Hybrid is acceptable.

AS:
Technology stack preferences?

RM:
No strict preference, but it should be future-proof and easy to maintain.

AS:
Timeline?

RM:
MVP in 4–6 months focusing on new connections and service requests.

AS:
This has been extremely insightful, Rajiv. We'll consolidate these inputs and come back with a solution approach, phased roadmap, and architecture.

RM:
Great. This platform is a strategic priority for us.

AS:
We understand. Thanks again for your time.

RM:
Thank you, Anita. Looking forward to the proposal. Bye.
